# Faith Should Raise the Bar on Due Diligence Rather Than Replace It

Faith-driven real estate has grown into a recognizable corner of the private investment market. Operators increasingly lead with their values, and investors who share those values are often drawn to them. That alignment can be meaningful. It can also, if investors are not careful, become a substitute for the scrutiny any investment deserves.

 Steven Libman, founder of Investing With Purpose™, a faith-driven multifamily real estate firm, holds a clear position on this. Shared convictions are a reason to look more closely at an operator, not a reason to look less closely.

 “The faith-driven aspect should raise your due diligence, not replace it,” says Libman. “Faith-driven operators still need to be excellent operators.”

 The Same Questions Libman’s starting point is that a faith-driven operator should be able to answer every question asked of any other sponsor. That means the terms of its debt, the size of its reserves, the length and depth of its track record, and the kinds of properties it is pursuing today.

 He places particular weight on one question: what was the operator’s most difficult deal, and how did it communicate with investors and lenders while working through it? An operator’s conduct under pressure, in his view, says more about future behavior than any single success.

 The Cost of Faith One question Libman welcomes more than any other is what the faith component actually costs. Investors ask it in two ways. Some want to know the financial cost of on-site care programming at the property level. Others want to know whether a faith-forward identity limits who will partner with the firm.

 On the property-level question, his answer is direct. At Investing With Purpose, the cost of the Purposed CARE Initiative is built into the underwriting from the start. Care coordinators are accounted for within the vacancy assumption, since no operator underwrites a property to full occupancy, and community events are budgeted as a defined operating line.

 “We should be able to name it, put a number to it, and show the operational upside of it,” says Libman. “If a faith-driven operator can’t explain what its mission costs and how it supports the business plan, that is worth a closer look.”

 Shared Language, Shared Responsibility The more subtle risk Libman describes involves language itself. Communities of faith share a vocabulary, and hearing that vocabulary from an operator can create a sense of trust before any due diligence has been done.

 “When we speak the same language, it can lower our guard,” he says. “We assume someone who shares our values will operate the way we would. Alignment on values matters, but it doesn’t answer how the business plan will be executed.”

 Libman is careful to say that the vast majority of people who speak openly about their faith do so sincerely, and he does not believe faith-driven operators should be viewed with suspicion for doing so. His point is that sincerity and operational capability are separate questions, and both deserve an answer.

 That distinction shows up most clearly in the investment materials themselves. A sensitivity analysis should model downside scenarios with the same rigor any institutional sponsor would apply. Shared beliefs can open a conversation, but they cannot stand in for a plan.

 Testing the Plan For Libman, the framework comes down to separating two kinds of judgment. Investors can and should evaluate whether an operator’s core values align with their own. What they are testing in due diligence is something else: the investment thesis, the operating plan, and the operator’s ability to deliver both.

 “Scripture tells us it is God’s job to judge the heart,” says Libman. “What we are responsible for testing is the investment thesis and whether it aligns with our core values.”

 As more faith-driven operators enter the market, that discipline is likely to become an important way for investors to distinguish mission-driven operators who also run excellent businesses from those still building that capability.
