# No Lift Lines, No Membership: Why Telluride Feels Like a Private Ski Club

Telluride draws a fraction of the skiers who crowd Colorado’s big-name resorts. For longtime local broker Rick Fusting, that space is the town’s real luxury, and this El Niño winter may be the season to discover it.

 Rick Fusting has already been skiing. Weeks before the lifts start spinning for the season, he was high on the mountain above tree line, where early storms have been stacking up snow even as the town below sees rain.

 “There’s a lot of snow up high,” says Fusting, a Telluride real estate broker who has lived in town since 1995. “I’d say the snowpack is about 300% above normal above tree line. We’re off to a really good start. We just need colder temperatures.”

 A strong early season is good news for any ski town. But ask Fusting what makes Telluride worth the trip, and he won’t start with snowfall totals. He’ll start with what you won’t find here: the crowds.

 “It is like our own private ski club,” he says, “and the only dues are that you have to ski in the most beautiful place in the U.S.”

 The crowd math Telluride’s quiet slopes start with geography. Colorado’s busiest resorts sit within an easy drive of Denver. Telluride is roughly six hours away by car, and longer when it snows.

 “There’s no drive-up traffic from a major city, so the lines are nonexistent,” Fusting says. Instead of weekend day-trippers, Telluride draws destination travelers who fly in and typically stay about five days.

 The resorts’ own planning numbers show the gap. According to Telluride’s master development plan, an average core-season day brings about 3,900 skiers, and the busiest day of each season has averaged around 7,600. The plan notes that long lift lines are uncommon on most weekdays and non-peak weekends.

 Breckenridge, among the busiest ski areas in the country, is designed to comfortably handle about 17,800 skiers a day. Its busiest days have topped 20,000.

 For a skier, that gap changes the whole shape of the day. “Skiing is expensive, and people want to get their money’s worth,” Fusting says. “So they ski bell to bell, nine to four. At most resorts, they spend a lot of that day standing in lift lines.”

 In Telluride, he says, you can often ski up to a lift on the expert terrain and find fewer than 20 people waiting. Holiday weeks bring more skiers to the beginner and intermediate lifts, but the overall pace stays easy. “Most skiers are tired after a half day,” he says, “because they don’t waste their time standing in line.”

 The private-club feeling, with a lift ticket Over the past several years, private ski clubs have become one of the defining trends among the ultra-wealthy. At places like Montana’s Yellowstone Club and Utah’s Powder Haven, membership is tied to owning real estate in the community, and members share the mountain with relatively few other skiers.

 Fusting understands the appeal. “Very wealthy people buy time,” he says. “They buy privacy and exclusivity. They don’t want to wait in lines. It’s the same reason they fly private: they don’t want to spend their whole day in an airport.”

 Telluride, he argues, delivers much of that experience without the membership. “Anybody can buy a lift ticket in Telluride and ski like it’s a private resort, because there just aren’t many people here,” he says.

 He has watched buyers weigh the two. About four years ago, Fusting worked with a client who was set to become one of the first members of a new private ski club being built in Utah. The buyer chose Telluride instead. “He thought it was far more beautiful,” Fusting says.

 There was a social reason, too. Private clubs typically limit how many guests a member can bring, which can make it hard to ski with friends. “In Telluride, anybody can ski with anybody,” Fusting says. “Your friends just buy a lift ticket.”

 A powder day, Fusting-style Ask Fusting to describe his ideal ski day and he starts early. On a powder morning, he’s in line by 8:40 with his wife and friends, riding Chair 8 and then Chair 9 up to Telluride’s expert terrain.

 “It’s all north- and northeast-facing, so the quality of the snow is really good,” he says. He’ll ski untracked powder for the first four or five runs, then move over to Chair 6 for a few more while he waits for Gold Hill to open.

 Gold Hill sits above tree line, catches a little more snow and offers more interesting terrain. After three or four runs there, Fusting and his group ski down to town for a beer at Oak. “That’s the classic fun day,” he says.

 Finding his friends on the mountain takes no planning. “If I ride up Chair 8 and Chair 9, I’ll just run into my friends up there,” he says. “You don’t have to set a meeting point, because it’s not very crowded.”

 The half-day advantage Short lines change the economics of a ski day. Skiers don’t have to stay out until the lifts close to feel they got their money’s worth. “You can ski in the morning, say ‘I’m tired,’ and then do something with the rest of your day,” Fusting says. “You don’t feel like you have to be out there all day long.”

 That leaves room for lunch on the mountain or an early après. Fusting’s pick is Alpino Vino, a European-style hut near the top of Gold Hill. At 11,966 feet, the resort bills it as the highest-elevation fine-dining restaurant in North America.

 The hut began as a private retreat on a historic mining claim. Today it serves an alpine Italian lunch menu of handmade pastas, panini and antipasti, with views across the Wilson Range. Tables are limited, so it’s a spot for a memorable meal rather than a lingering afternoon.

 “It’s a really cool, super high-end spot, and the food is amazing,” Fusting says. “You can ski all morning and then spend an hour at Alpino Vino.”

 Why this winter is different This season adds one more reason to look at Telluride. Forecasters expect one of the strongest El Niño events on record, with NOAA putting the odds of a very strong El Niño this fall and winter at roughly 90%.

 In El Niño years, the storm track tends to dip south, steering Pacific moisture into the Southwest and the southern Rockies. The San Juan Mountains around Telluride are often among the first big ranges those storms hit.

 “Telluride looks to be in the bullseye of this year’s storms,” Fusting says. “Further north, in Wyoming and British Columbia, it’ll probably be a little drier.”

 The moisture has already arrived. In late October, the remnants of Hurricane Trudy brought heavy rain and serious flooding to towns south of Telluride, including Durango and Pagosa Springs. Higher up, the same storms have been building the early snowpack Fusting skied.

 Forecasters caution that El Niño shifts the odds rather than guaranteeing a big winter. OpenSnow’s review of past El Niño winters found Telluride tends to land near normal snowfall, with the potential for a stronger start and finish to the season.

 Fusting expects the buzz alone to move bookings. “Everyone is saying El Niño is coming to the southern United States,” he says. “So people are going to book, and lodging is going to fill up quickly.”

 He saw a version of it this August in Chile, where he traveled to take advantage of an El Niño-boosted season that brought exceptional snow. “The lodging was all booked up and super expensive,” he says. His advice for anyone eyeing Telluride this winter: plan early.

 Beyond the resort boundary For skiers who want even more solitude, Fusting points to the terrain beyond the lifts. Heli-skiing is available right in town: Telluride Helitrax, operating since 1982, flies guests into more than 200 square miles of the surrounding San Juan Mountains.

 The more adventurous can also catch a helicopter from Telluride over the range to Silverton for a day of steep, expert-only skiing.

 Remote, but no longer hard to reach Telluride’s distance from big cities is what keeps it quiet, but getting there is easier than it once was. Private planes and Denver Air Connection flights land at Telluride Regional Airport, minutes from town.

 Most visitors fly into Montrose Regional Airport (MTJ), about 75 minutes away. This winter it offers nonstop service from hubs including Denver, Dallas, Houston, Phoenix, Atlanta and Newark. “It’s a pretty big hub on the Western Slope,” Fusting says, “and it’s growing.”

 Once in town, many visitors never need a car. Telluride is walkable, and a free gondola links the town with Mountain Village.

 Room for every kind of skier In Fusting’s experience, uncrowded slopes shape how people use their homes on the mountain. With a ski-in, ski-out home, everyone from first-timers to experts can ski right out the door.

 “With no lift lines, a family can all stay together,” he says. “They don’t get separated or lost.”

 The mountain’s layout helps. Beginner, intermediate and expert terrain are each grouped in their own areas, which Fusting says is rare among ski resorts. That means beginners can learn on green runs close to home without expert skiers zipping past them, he says.

 The real membership Fusting arrived in Telluride in 1995 and never left. Asked why, he doesn’t talk about powder or lift lines.

 “I stay here for the community,” he says. “It’s a solid community of people who care for each other. And it’s one of the most beautiful places in the world. When you wake up and look outside, you’re always thankful for that, and respectful of it.”

 Private clubs sell space, time and a sense of belonging. In Telluride, Fusting has found all three, and the price of admission is a lift ticket.
