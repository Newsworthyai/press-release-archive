# What Florida’s Tightening Audit Rules Mean for Self-Managed HOA Boards

Florida has quietly raised the bar on HOA financial accountability, and many self-managed boards are not yet operating at the level the state now requires. The change is specific: associations above a certain revenue threshold can no longer waive their annual full audit requirement every year. Under the updated rules, a waiver is only available every other year – meaning communities that have been waiving annually are now out of step with current Florida law.

 Clayton Thompson, co-founder of HOA Start, a software platform built for self-managed associations, sees this shift as part of a broader pattern of Florida raising expectations for HOA governance – and volunteer boards being the last to find out.

 “Florida is making these types of platforms mandatory because there needs to be a system of record where people can log in and review the financials,” Thompson said. “The boards that are behind on this aren’t bad boards. They just haven’t had anyone tell them the rules changed.”

 What the Audit Rule Change Actually Requires The updated requirement applies to associations above approximately $500,000 in annual revenue. For those communities, a full audit – which can run between $7,000 and $12,000 for a medium-sized association – is now required at minimum every other year, regardless of whether the membership votes to waive it. A compilation or review may still satisfy the off years, but the full audit can no longer be deferred indefinitely through annual member votes.

 For boards that have been operating under the assumption that a member vote always settles the question, the practical implication is immediate: the audit cost needs to be budgeted for, whether or not it falls in the current fiscal year. Communities that discover mid-year that a full audit is required – and have not set aside the funds – face a gap that either comes out of reserves or requires a special assessment.

 The audit rule sits within a wider set of Florida transparency obligations under Florida Statutes 720 and 718, which also require HOAs to maintain accessible records online and to produce documents in response to formal homeowner requests within defined timeframes. For volunteer boards managing records across personal laptops, shared drives, and email inboxes, those timelines create real operational strain.

 Records Readiness as an Everyday Practice Thompson describes the records request scenario in practical terms. A homeowner files a formal request. The board needs to respond within the statutory window. The question is whether the right people know where the documents live – and whether they can get to them without depending on whoever created the file originally.

 “When there’s a system in place and someone requests a document, you have confidence within your board to be able to say, I know where that lives,” Thompson said. “It’s as simple as that. You go get it and send it off. That’s gaining trust.”

 The strain, he argues, is not usually about unwillingness to comply. It is about infrastructure. A board managing its records informally is one resignation, one illness, or one hard drive failure away from not being able to produce what the law requires on the timeline the law sets.

 Beyond individual requests, Thompson points to the value of a documented decision trail for boards facing homeowner challenges. When a board has consistently recorded its decisions, meeting minutes, and approvals in a central system, it can demonstrate a track record of good-faith governance that stands on its own. “You can go back and say, we’ve consistently said this,” he said. “Here’s the board meeting from a year ago. Here’s the one from last quarter. We’ve made the same decision every time this has come up.”
