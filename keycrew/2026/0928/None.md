# True Ski-In/Ski-Out Is Rare in Vail, and the Reason Is Structural

The phrase “ski-in, ski-out” carries an obvious appeal, and buyers arriving in Vail, Colorado, often assume it is both the goal and the norm. It is neither. True ski-in, ski-out is one of the rarest things in town, and the reason has less to do with price than with how the town and the mountain were built. Mark Gordon, a REALTOR® with Christiania Realty, spends much of his time resetting that expectation before a buyer ever tours a home.

 A Pedestrian Village by Design Vail was master-planned in the early 1960s as a European-style pedestrian village. The idea was that visitors would leave their cars in the parking structures along the frontage road, walk past the shops and restaurants, and reach the lifts on foot. That design gave the town its character, but it also meant very few homes were ever built directly on the snow. “There just isn’t a lot of true ski-in, ski-out in Vail or Lionshead,” Gordon says. “It’s all short walks.” What exists is concentrated and costly. Most everything else is ski-adjacent by nature.

 A Mountain on Federal Land The larger constraint is the land itself. Vail Mountain sits on national forest land, with the ski company operating as a long-term leaseholder rather than a landowner. It owns the lifts, the restaurants, and the infrastructure, but not the ground. That single fact means homes cannot be built on the slopes the way they are in places like Deer Valley, where private land allows houses between the runs. In Vail, all residential building happens down in the valley, on a strip roughly seven miles long and less than a mile wide. The result is a town with almost no sprawl and a fixed ceiling on how much true ski-in, ski-out inventory can ever exist. It is not a supply that grows.

 The Club Workaround That scarcity produced an innovation. Vail Resorts operates three signature clubs, one each at Golden Peak, Vail Village, and Lionshead, that function like country clubs for skiers. Members keep a family locker, have their skis brought to the lift, their boots dried overnight, and breakfast served in the morning, along with occasional early access to the mountain before it opens to the public. Demand is such that most clubs now carry waiting lists of around seven years. For a wide range of buyers, Gordon says, a club membership recreates much of the convenience of ski-in, ski-out without the address. More modest options exist too, from a la carte arrangements to seasonal ski lockers near the lifts, paired with the town’s free bus system, which Gordon describes as running with near-Japanese punctuality.

 The Price of Proximity Proximity to the lift remains the clearest driver of price. In a Vail Village base area, Gordon puts the figure at roughly $1.5 million to $2.5 million per bedroom. Move a few blocks out and it falls toward $1 million. Ride the bus route to East or West Vail and a two-bedroom condominium can be found closer to $1 million in total. That spread is what gives Vail its range of entry points, and it reframes the question for buyers fixed on skiing to their door.

 Gordon recalls a buyer who arrived certain he would accept nothing less than ski-in, ski-out. No home in Vail fit both that requirement and his needs, so during the search he joined a club and bought a larger house across the highway, with the views and space to host family and guests. He could not have had that home on the snow. For most buyers, Gordon says, the real trade is not ski-in, ski-out versus ski-adjacent. It is proximity versus the home itself, and the home usually wins.
