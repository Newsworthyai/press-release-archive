# What is a CAC Test, and Should you get one?

DALLAS - November 17, 2026 (NEWMEDIAWIRE) - A coronary artery calcium, or CAC, test is a quick, noninvasive CT scan that looks for calcium deposits in the coronary arteries. Calcium can be an early sign of plaque buildup, or atherosclerosis. The resulting “calcium score” can give individuals and their healthcare professionals another piece of information about future risk of cardiovascular disease or event such as heart attack or stroke. The scan takes about 10 to 15 minutes, does not require injected contrast dye and uses a low dose of radiation. But the American Heart Association emphasizes that CAC testing is not necessary for everyone.

 The American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology dyslipidemia guideline supports selective use of CAC scoring, particularly when a person's cardiovascular risk falls in a middle range and there is uncertainty about whether to begin or intensify cholesterol-lowering treatment. The 2026 guideline for medical professionals encourages the use of the American Heart Association’s PREVENT™ equations as the starting point for estimating cardiovascular risk and identifies CAC as a tool that can help refine treatment decisions when the answer remains unclear.

 “The American Heart Association does not recommend broad based use of the CAC test for screening. The Association recommends it as a decision aid for people at borderline or intermediate risk for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or heart and blood-vessel disease caused by the buildup of cholesterol-containing plaque in the arteries – but only when the decision about whether to use drug therapy to lower risk is uncertain. It is a tiebreaker test,” said Manesh R. Patel, M.D., FAHA, volunteer president of the American Heart Association, chief of the division of cardiology, chief of the division of pharmacology, the Richard Stack Distinguished Professor of Medicine at Duke University School of Medicine, a senior investigator at the Duke Clinical Research Institute, and vice president of Heart and Vascular Services for Duke Health, Durham, North Carolina.

 Recent research and media coverage have highlighted that important distinction: CAC provides the greatest value when the result can change a treatment decision. New studies have found that modern risk estimation with the Association’s PREVENT equations can identify coronary artery disease risk effectively for many people, while adding CAC information can be particularly useful for people in borderline (3% to less than 5%) and intermediate (5% to less than 10%) risk groups. The Association’s guidelines recommend using calcium findings to help inform decisions on the use of statins (a class of prescription medications that lower low-density lipoprotein, i.e. LDL or "bad" cholesterol, by reducing cholesterol production in the liver) for men age 40 and older and women age 45 and older with borderline or intermediate 10-year risk of heart attack or stroke.

 Four things consumers should know about CAC:

 * The first step is a conversation, not necessarily a scan. People should talk with their healthcare professional about their overall cardiovascular risk and whether knowing their CAC score would actually change what they do next, for example, if the best strategy to prevent a heart attack or stroke should include cholesterol-lowering medication such as a statin. It generally isn't useful when cardiovascular risk is already very low or when the appropriate treatment is already clear, such as for those with diabetes or very high cholesterol. It is also generally not helpful if someone has already had a heart attack or cardiac catheterization.
* It looks for evidence of plaque already in your coronary arteries. The scan, which is typically not covered by health insurance and can cost the patient $100 to $400, detects and measures calcium associated with plaque buildup. Typically, the higher the calcium score, the greater the potential for plaque burden and future cardiovascular risk.
* Think of it as a potential decision-making tool, not an annual screening test. The Association recommends that the “first prescription” for prevention should be lifestyle modification, including avoiding smoking and tobacco use and emphasizing a healthy diet, exercise and sleep as defined by the Association’s Life’s Essential 8 ™, the key measures for improving and maintaining cardiovascular health.
* A score of zero is reassuring, but it isn't a lifetime “all clear.” A CAC score of zero doesn’t rule out noncalcified plaque. Other factors that impact cardiovascular disease risk, including diabetes, smoking, very high cholesterol and a strong family history of premature heart disease, still matter.

 Learn more about coronary artery calcium testing (CAC test) from the American Heart Association. The Association’s consumer information explains who may benefit, who may not need the test, what happens during the scan and what to discuss with your healthcare professional.
