# When Everyone Around You Says Yes: Kari Ghanem on the Hidden Cost of Success

SPRINGFIELD, MO, September 24, 2026 /24-7PressRelease/ -- Success changes a room.  Early in a career, people tend to tell you when something isn't working. Customers say no. Colleagues challenge an idea. Someone questions the decision. The marketplace generally doesn't care much about anyone's ego.  But become successful enough and the room can begin behaving differently.  The company grows. The title gets bigger. More people depend on your decisions. Your reputation begins arriving before you do. People become a little more careful about what they say and how they say it.  Eventually, the person everybody turns to for answers can find themselves wondering who they can talk to when they don't have one.  Transformational coach Kari Ghanem knows that person well.  Ghanem works with entrepreneurs, business owners and high performers, many of whom have already accomplished what other people are still chasing. They aren't necessarily looking for someone to convince them they're capable of succeeding.  They've already proven that.  What they may not have is somewhere they can stop being the person everyone else expects them to be.  "Some of these people have been successful for years, and they're surrounded by people who tell them what they want to hear," Ghanem says. "They don't need another yes person. Sometimes they need somebody who can look at them and say, 'No. That's bull***t. Let's talk about what's really going on.'"  Ghanem is comfortable being that person.  A title, a bank account or the size of someone's company doesn't carry much weight once the work begins. In fact, those markers of achievement can sometimes make honest conversations more difficult to find.  Success can create insulation.  Employees may hesitate to challenge the person signing their checks. Friends and family know the role that person has always occupied. An executive who has built a reputation around strength can become exceptionally good at appearing certain even when something privately feels anything but certain.  "They're used to being the person everybody else looks to," explains Ghanem. "Where do they go when they want to let their walls down? Where do they get to take the armor off and just be honest? A lot of successful people don't have many places where they can do that."  For Ghanem, taking off the armor doesn't mean finding another person willing to nod along.  Her version of compassion has teeth.  Listening is part of the work, and so is getting into the trenches with a client. But Ghanem isn't afraid to make someone uncomfortable, question the story they're telling themselves or point out when their words and actions no longer line up.  Then comes the harder part: doing something with what has been uncovered.  "I tell people before they work with me that I'm going to piss their ego off," Ghanem says. "They're always a little shocked when it actually happens, but I warned them. I'm not here to impress you. If we're working together, we're going to be honest."  That bluntness could easily be mistaken for the entirety of Ghanem's approach.  It isn't.  Some of the deepest conversations happen because Ghanem is equally willing to be open about herself. Her clients don't encounter someone pretending to have lived a flawless life. She speaks candidly about her own struggles, mistakes and difficult chapters, believing that openness gives other people permission to stop performing.  She adds: "When I'm open about my own life, people start opening up too. Sometimes they're almost shocked by what they're telling me. I'm not shocked. I've lived a lot of life. You're not going to scare me away because you tell me something that's messy."  For someone accustomed to maintaining an image, that can be disarming.  There is also a difference between challenging someone's ego and humiliating them. The point isn't to win an argument or prove that the coach is the smartest person in the room. It's to get past the performance long enough to see what is actually there.  Sometimes what is there is an identity that has outlived its usefulness.  A founder may have learned years ago that control was how things got done. Someone who survived difficult circumstances may have learned never to depend on anybody. A high performer may have spent decades measuring themselves by what they could accomplish.  Those patterns may have worked extraordinarily well.  That's what makes questioning them difficult.  "Sometimes the thing that got you here is the exact thing that's keeping you stuck," Ghanem shares. "Nobody wants to hear that when what they've been doing has made them successful. But at some point you have to ask yourself, is this still working for me, or am I just playing the same character because I've gotten really good at playing it?"  Ghanem has an interesting way of describing what can happen next. People, she says, can "master manipulate" themselves into believing the character they've created is simply who they are.  The successful one. The strong one. The person who fixes everything. The one who doesn't need anybody.  Stay inside a role long enough and it can become difficult to tell where the role ends and the person begins.  That is one reason Ghanem prefers the term "transformational coach" to "motivational coach."  Temporary inspiration doesn't interest her much. Neither does a coaching culture that can make dramatic change sound as simple as completing a course, attending a weekend event or finding the right collection of slogans.  "This isn't for the faint of heart," she says. "It's not, 'Take a course and now you've reached this level.' No. This is something you choose every day. Sometimes transformation is painful. Sometimes you see something about yourself that you really don't want to see. Then you have to decide what you're going to do with it."  That last part matters.  A coach can ask the uncomfortable question, challenge the pattern and create enough trust for someone to finally say what they haven't been saying. But Ghanem doesn't believe anyone can do another person's work for them.  What happens after the realization still belongs to the person sitting across from her.  "I can get in the trenches with you. I'll push you. I'll support you. I'll celebrate the wins with you," Ghanem says. "But I'm not going to live your life for you. At some point, you have to be willing to do the work."  It's a distinction that comes from experience.  Caring about someone doesn't require carrying what belongs to them. Helping doesn't mean protecting another person from every uncomfortable consequence or taking responsibility for decisions that remain theirs to make.  For someone whose natural instinct is to help, that realization hasn't always been easy.  But it has influenced the way Ghanem thinks about transformation: there has to be agency on both sides.  "I don't want people who are looking for a quick fix," explains Ghanem. "I want the person who's already put in work in their life and is ready to go deeper. Bring me the truth. Bring me the ugly stuff. Bring me the thing you haven't wanted to say out loud. We can work with that. But we can't do anything with a performance."  That philosophy is particularly relevant to people who have become exceptionally good at performing success.  Accomplishment doesn't automatically answer the questions underneath it.  A business can grow while the person running it feels stuck. Someone can continue achieving while privately wondering why it doesn't feel the way it once did. The person everybody admires can look at the life they've built and still wonder whether it resembles the life they actually want.  Ghanem isn't interested in telling those people they should want less.  She wants to know what they want now.  "I love working with people who have already accomplished things," she says. "I'm not trying to take that away from them. I'm asking, okay, you've done all of this. Now what? What do you actually want? Not what you're supposed to want. Not what looks good. What do you want?"  That question may explain Ghanem's work better than any coaching label could.  The point isn't to turn accomplished people into someone else.  It's to ask whether the person they've become is still someone they consciously chose.  And sometimes answering that requires something money, status and achievement cannot guarantee:  Someone in the room who cares enough not to say yes.
