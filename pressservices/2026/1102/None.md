# One Up Outlines Practical IoT Security Steps for Oregon Manufacturer

One Up Solutions Northwest Shares Guidance for Protecting Connected Factory Equipment on a Budget

 Tigard, United States - November 2, 2026 / One Up Solutions Northwest, Inc. /

 New operational technology blueprint provides small and mid-sized manufacturers with cost-effective security strategies for legacy factory-floor machinery.

10/23/2026 TIGARD, OR - Small and mid-sized manufacturers across the Pacific Northwest face an escalating challenge: securing connected operational technology (OT) that does not support standard corporate antivirus or office security software. To address this critical vulnerability, One Up Solutions Northwest has launched a specialized framework targeting affordable Internet of Things (IoT) security specifically tailored for the factory floor.

The initiative provides actionable blueprints for regional businesses working with legacy machinery and tight cybersecurity budgets. Instead of demanding expensive equipment overhauls or disruptive software patches, the focus shifts to securing the digital perimeter surrounding existing assets.

Navigating the Extended Lifecycle of Industrial Equipment

Industrial hardware often operates on a starkly different lifecycle than traditional corporate IT infrastructure. While factory-floor sensors and controllers physically last for decades, the internal software powering them frequently ages out of vendor security support.

Applying standard office maintenance routines to active production networks can cause unexpected downtime and scheduling conflicts. True resilience requires direct coordination between IT professionals and the operations team managing the physical production schedules. For teams navigating these initial concepts, anchoring the foundational vocabulary is an essential first step. Manufacturers can clarify these definitions by reviewing the Operational Glossary: What Does IoT Stand for in Manufacturing?.

Smarter Network Segmentation: Isolation is Not the Answer

A cornerstone of modern factory security is separating operational networks from corporate and guest Wi-Fi. However, heavy-handed isolation can cripple the real-time data flow needed for modern logistics.

"Network segmentation doesn't mean total isolation," says Dave Chapman, President of One Up Solutions Northwest. "Connected equipment must still safely communicate with supervisory systems and verified cloud platforms. Success lies in mapping out exactly what data a device needs to send, and systematically blocking everything else through existing network infrastructure."

By establishing strict communication boundaries, manufacturing plants can keep essential production visible and manageable without exposing vulnerable machinery to broader internet threats.

Strengthening Procurement and Access Controls

Securing the perimeter means nothing if default settings are left wide open. The blueprint stresses immediate changes to default factory credentials during initial equipment setup, alongside secure, restricted storage for authorization keys on shared industrial interfaces.

Furthermore, hardware security begins long before a device reaches the assembly line. Pacific Northwest manufacturers are encouraged to implement rigorous supplier checks, verifying component traceability and maintaining meticulous documentation for all incoming parts. These purchasing logs serve as the foundation for future technical audits and maintenance tracking. To help business owners understand how these factors interact, the firm has compiled a detailed analysis of the primary Security Challenges Impacting Industrial IoT Operations.

Adapting Network Monitoring to Production Realities

When legacy devices cannot host modern security software, tracking overall network behavior becomes a powerful defensive alternative. Flagging unexpected server destinations, spikes in data volume, or altered communication intervals allows managers to catch potential breaches early.

Crucially, ongoing security assessments must align with standard factory workflows. Routine maintenance windows should be scheduled concurrently with product changeovers or planned equipment downtime to preserve maximum operational efficiency.

Aligning Device Procurement with True Operational Need

Modern manufacturing relies on a diverse ecosystem of specialized technology, including vibration sensors, RFID tracking bands, environmental monitors, and automated vision inspection systems. Because these tools serve entirely different roles, procurement should be driven by explicit physical needs rather than broad technical trends.

Rather than chasing generalized production promises, managers should evaluate every new asset based on its intended operational environment, its approved connection pathways, and long-term technical support responsibilities.
